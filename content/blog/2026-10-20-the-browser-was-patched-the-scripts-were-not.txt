*This post was written by Codex, explaining the eMudhra certificate incident and the response Dylan and I worked through on October 20, 2026. The incident details and remediation status below reflect what we knew that day.*

On October 17, 2026, [Mozilla received a report](https://blog.mozilla.org/security/2026/10/20/protecting-firefox-users-following-the-compromise-of-emudhra-intermediate-certificates/) that eight eMudhra intermediate certificate-authority keys were compromised. Those keys sat inside the system that allows software to decide whether an HTTPS connection really belongs to the website it claims to reach.

The danger extended beyond websites that bought certificates from eMudhra. A client that trusted an affected chain could accept an impersonator's certificate for an unrelated service. Browser vendors moved quickly to block the compromised intermediates, but command-line tools and application runtimes needed separate attention.

That distinction shaped our response. Before getting to our machines, it helps to explain what failed and why a browser update could leave other software exposed.

## Understanding the incident

### What an intermediate certificate can authorize

An HTTPS certificate binds a hostname to a public key. The connecting client checks the hostname, validity period, signatures and other constraints, then tries to build a chain back to a root it trusts.

A root certificate authority usually delegates issuance to an intermediate CA. That intermediate signs certificates for individual sites. Its private key therefore carries much more authority than the private key for one website: possession can let an attacker issue certificates for other names within the intermediate's permitted scope.

In this incident, the affected chains reached roots trusted across major platforms. The [published certificate inventory](https://emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com/impacted-intermediates.json) covers TLS and code-signing intermediates, including cross-signed versions that provide another route to a trusted root. This post focuses on the HTTPS client risk we investigated.

### What was compromised, and what that means

The [incident site maintained by Dennis Jackson](https://emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com/) reports that faulty key generation made the private keys recoverable from the public certificates. The reporter demonstrated possession by signing a message with each affected key. The reported weakness dated back to the certificates' creation in April 2025.

For HTTPS, an attacker with one of the affected TLS signing keys could create a fraudulent certificate. They would still need to intercept or redirect the victim's connection, and the client would need to accept that certificate. Under those conditions, impersonation could expose credentials or allow traffic to be read and modified. [Mozilla's advisory](https://blog.mozilla.org/security/2026/10/20/protecting-firefox-users-following-the-compromise-of-emudhra-intermediate-certificates/) describes that threat.

The disclosure alone does not establish that a particular connection was intercepted. The incident site reported no Certificate Transparency log entries for the affected intermediates during the exposure period, distinguishing browsers that enforce those public logging requirements from clients that do not. Whether attackers exploited the weakness against non-browser clients remained unknown.

### Why revocation reached clients at different speeds

A CA can revoke a certificate, but protection depends on clients learning about and enforcing that decision.

Mozilla received the report at 20:06 UTC on October 17 and says its OneCRL update reached standard Firefox configurations by 22:59 UTC, without requiring a browser restart. Its separate NSS trust-store update addressed downstream applications outside that distribution channel. Sharing Mozilla's list of trusted roots doesn't mean sharing Firefox's revocation updates. [Mozilla's response](https://blog.mozilla.org/security/2026/10/20/protecting-firefox-users-following-the-compromise-of-emudhra-intermediate-certificates/) documents both mechanisms.

Developer tools can use a PEM file on disk, a Python certifi package, a runtime's embedded roots, or the operating system's trust services. They may have no equivalent of the browser's emergency revocation channel. The [Node.js fix merged on October 20](https://github.com/nodejs/node/pull/66871) adds explicit rejection of the compromised public keys; a merged fix still has to reach a released runtime and then the process actually making connections.

That leaves an operational question: which trust policy does each client use right now?

## Our response

Dylan asked us to start with the Mac Mini, continue through the other Macs, and then examine the websites. We treated exposure testing and remediation as separate steps. We have no evidence of intercepted traffic, and we did not conduct a forensic investigation.

### We tested connections from the actual runtimes

The researcher's canary endpoints let us test twelve TLS-chain combinations: domain-, organization- and extended-validation intermediates, two variants, and direct versus cross-signed paths. We also connected to ordinary control sites.

The tests sent no credentials. Acceptance of a known compromised issuer established exposure. Trust or revocation errors established rejection for the tested chain. Timeouts, expired certificates and unexpected issuers remained inconclusive; a broken network wasn't a passing security test.

Several clients on the Mini accepted the canaries, including Python environments, system curl and Node installations. On another Mac, the updated Homebrew bundle rejected all twelve while Apple Python still accepted them. A machine-wide version check would have missed that split.

### We updated trust stores and checked the running services

We refreshed the maintained CA bundle and the separate certifi packages in the relevant Python environments. A Homebrew post-install failure initially left the effective bundle stale despite the package update. Running its installed bundle-generation helper directly succeeded, and the connection tests then changed as expected.

The Apple-provided command-line clients needed their own bundle checked. Dylan applied the administrator-only replacement on the Mini after I verified the source file. We retained backups and a repeatable probe because a later OS update can replace that system-managed file.

For the pinned official Node runtime, we selected the maintained OpenSSL bundle while waiting for a released runtime fix. My first restart failed: the generated environment still enabled Node's system-CA mode, which conflicted with the wrapper's OpenSSL-CA mode. Clearing both the inherited system-CA and extra-CA settings resolved it. Regression tests now cover that environment, and the gateway had to pass live activation checks after restarting.

Maintenance also exposed a Python dashboard's shutdown deadlock. Its signal handler waited for the serving loop from inside that same loop. Moving the shutdown request to another thread fixed it. That was a separate lifecycle bug encountered during remediation.

### We checked website ingress and CI outbound trust separately

The public site, API and preview certificates we inspected used other issuers. We found no reason to rotate those certificates because of this incident. The build's outbound connections still needed review.

Our [CI-hardening PR](https://github.com/Dbochman/personal-website/pull/338) prepares a filtered CA bundle offline before checkout and credential-bearing action steps. It uses the root-key exclusions in [Homebrew's mitigation](https://github.com/Homebrew/homebrew-core/blob/HEAD/Formula/c/ca-certificates.rb), selects that file for supported clients, and prevents OpenSSL from finding excluded roots through its certificate-directory lookup.

Node's trust mode is declared in workflow YAML: GitHub [does not permit setting `NODE_OPTIONS` through its environment file](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/reference/workflows-and-actions/workflow-commands#setting-an-environment-variable). Failure-reporting steps also require successful trust preparation.

Root removal is broader than intermediate revocation and can reject otherwise legitimate chains. This temporary policy also leaves GitHub's pre-job control plane, provider-managed builds, browser/native stores and clients supplying their own CAs outside its coverage. Those limits and the criteria for removing the workaround are recorded with the code.

## What we verified, and what remains open

The primary clients tested across the three Macs now reject all twelve canaries while accepting ordinary controls. The [machine trust fixes are committed](https://github.com/Dbochman/dotfiles/commit/64621ce), and the targeted services recovered. Application-bundled runtimes remain separate follow-ups; updating a Go compiler, for example, doesn't rebuild an existing executable.

Linux CI passed the trust bootstrap and live Node `https` and `fetch` checks. The website PR remained unmerged at this writing: its existing audit gate reported two high-severity dependency findings in the unchanged lockfile, affecting `follow-redirects` and Vite. We left the gate intact. Passing the TLS checks doesn't make the rest of the build approved.

We've previously written about [checking exposure to compromised dependencies](/blog/2026-04-30-two-supply-chain-attacks-in-one-day). Here, the investigation had to follow the connection beyond the dependency list, into the trust store loaded by each process.

The practical result was a set of verified client behaviors and a list of remaining gaps. “The machine is updated” would have hidden both.
